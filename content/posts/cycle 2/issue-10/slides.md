## `Slide 2: THE ICON`
 
**Headline:** In April 2026, a miniskirt got read as a sign the economy was *thriving*.
 
Supporting line: Sabrina Carpenter took the Coachella stage in custom Dior. Soon after, a jump in miniskirt popularity was being read online as good news for the stock market. Nobody asked the skirt. The idea has a name, and it's older than your grandmother's stockings: the hemline index.

## `Slide 3: THE STAT`
 
**Headline:** Longer skirts meant a recession. A year later, shorter skirts meant a *boom*.
 
Supporting line: In March 2025, maxi skirt popularity jumped **393%** in a week, and got read online as a recession warning. In April 2026, miniskirts rose **78%**, and got read as the economy looking up. Opposite lengths, opposite verdicts, both in spring. One reading used a week of online data. The study that actually tested the idea used almost **90 years** of hemlines.
 
Citation: (Lapuerta, 2025, 2026; van Baardwijk & Franses, 2010)
 
*(footer: 2/9)*

## `Slide 4: THE ORIGIN`
 
**Headline:** The man credited with the hemline index was writing about *stockings*.
 
Supporting line: In **1929**, a Wharton PhD student studied why stocking sales boomed in the 1920s. One reason: shorter skirts put more leg on show, so women bought better stockings, more often. That's it. That's the theory. He never said skirts could predict the economy. That part got added later, somewhere between the retelling and the reposting.
 
Citation: (Taylor, 1929, pp. 27, 115)
 
Visual note: Include a picture of George W. Taylor

## `Slide 5: THE TURN`
 
**Headline:** When researchers tested it, the skirts were always *late*.
 
Supporting line: In 2010, two economists coded almost **90 years** of hemlines from a French fashion magazine. The economy did shape skirt lengths, but about **3 years** later, once trends had time to be designed, made and worn. When they checked whether skirts could warn of a recession, they found nothing. One of them now calls the hemline index an urban legend.
 
Citation: (Van Baardwijk & Franses, 2010; Het Financieele Dagblad, 2025)
 
*(footer: 4/9)*

## `Slide 6: THE MECHANISM`
 
**Headline:** Every spring, maxi skirts. In April 2026, also *sofas*.
 
Supporting line: Maxi skirt searches are about 2x higher in May than in January, every single year. That's the season, not a recession. In the week of 12 April 2026, Google searches for miniskirts jumped. However, so did maxi skirts, jeans and sofas, all in the same week (pizza didn't move). When everything spikes at once, it isn't a hemline. It's a data blip.
 
Chart 1: Google search interest, US, Jan 2022 to Sep 2026: mini skirt, jeans, sofa, pizza (each on its own scale), dashed line at the week of 12 April 2026
 
Citation: (Google, 2026)

## `Slide 7: THE COST`
 
**Headline:** Fashion might follow the market. But the market isn't *most* people's money.
 
Supporting line: For every $100 of US stocks, about $51 belongs to the richest 1% of households. The bottom half hold about 60 cents, between all of them. In 1989, the top 1% held about $43. So when a skirt gets read as good news for the stock market, the obvious follow-up is: “Good news for whom?”
 
Chart (from 10.1, section 2.2): who owns $100 of US stocks, Q2 2026 (top 1%, next 9%, middle 40%, bottom 50%)
 
Citation: (Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, 2026)

## `Slide 8: THE FORECAST`
 
**Headline:** Some wardrobes have nothing left to *cut*.
 
Supporting line: When the economy turned in 2008 and 2020, middle and higher-income households cut clothing spending by **13%** to **35%**. The lowest-earning 20% barely changed theirs. Likely not because times were good: at around **$800** a year, there may not be much left to cut. The downturn showed up elsewhere. At the 2020 peak, about **1 in 5** adults without a high school diploma were out of work, compared with about **1 in 12** with a degree.
 
Forecast line: Our call: the next time a trend gets read as a recession signal, the people it hits hardest won't be the ones changing their style.
 
Chart (from 10.2, section 2.3): change in clothing spending by income group, 2008 to 2010 and 2020 (percentage change)
 
Citation: (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025, 2026)

## `Slide 9: THE TAKE`
 
**Headline:** Long story short: the skirt was *never* the signal.
 
Supporting line: It’s a fun idea and it’ll be back every spring. But the people a downturn reaches first won’t be the ones changing their hemlines, they show up in the jobs data.
 
Closing line: For some of us it’s a trend. For the rest, it hits before the skirts do.
