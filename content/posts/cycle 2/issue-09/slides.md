## `Slide 2 — THE ICON`
 
**Headline:** In 1954 they were *just* jeans. In 2025 one pair sold for **$52,000**.

Supporting line: Marilyn Monroe wore them in a western and shared them with her stunt double. At first, they were just ordinary shop-bought denim. Then in 1999, they went to auction with two other pairs of jeans that Marilyn owned and became an investment. Later, in 2025, they sold again at auction.

## `Slide 3 — THE STAT`
 
**Headline:** While the jeans got famous, the job of *making* jeans was disappearing. 

Supporting line: In 2000, about 504,000 people worked in US clothing factories. By 2010, about 161,000. Roughly 94 jobs gone every day for a decade, and the fall had started years earlier.
 
Chart 1: US clothing factory jobs, 1990 to 2026, with the 2000s shaded.
Citation: (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, n.d.-a)

## `Slide 4 — THE MECHANISM`
 
**Headline:** Manufacturing jeans *didn't stop*, it *moved*.
Supporting line: About 97 in 100 pieces of clothing sold in the US now come from somewhere else. The sewing went to countries where the same work costs for far less. Nobody held a meeting, the industry just did the same math and ended up with the same solution.

## `Slide 5 — THE PRICE`
 
**Headline:** Here is the part that helped *you*. By 2010 clothes cost about the same as in 1990. Everything else cost about **71%** more.

Supporting line: By 2010, clothes had gone back down to what they cost in 1990, while everything else cost ~71% more. That gap was real money back in shopper’s pockets and it mattered the most to those with the least to spare. Several things kept prices down, including cheaper sewing and sewing is still done by hand, by someone.
 
Chart 2: Three lines starting together in 1990: prices of everyday stuff, prices of clothes, and US sewing jobs, 1990 to 2026.
Citation: (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, n.d.-a, n.d.-b, n.d.-c)

## `Slide 6 — THE COST`

**Headline:** So who sews them *now*?

Supporting line: Mostly workers overseas. In Bangladesh, one of the world's biggest clothing exporters, the legal minimum for a garment worker is about $101 a month. A US sewing machine operator on typical pay earns that in under six hours. At that minimum, the $52,000 jeans add up to about **43 years** of pay.

Chart 3: Two-figure comparison, years of pay equal to the jeans. US sewing machine operator on typical pay, about a year and a half. Bangladeshi garment worker at the legal minimum, about 43 years.
Citation: (Andaloro, 2025; Paul & Jamkhandikar, 2023; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026). 12,500 taka converted to USD at 1 USD = 123.17 BDT, 8 October 2026.
